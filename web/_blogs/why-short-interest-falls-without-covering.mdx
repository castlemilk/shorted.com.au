# Why ASX Short Interest Can Fall Without Short Covering

A stock's reported short interest falls from 10% to 8%. It is tempting to say that a fifth of the shorts have closed. That conclusion needs another number: the shares on issue.

If the company issued more shares while the reported short quantity stayed unchanged, the percentage would fall without any reduction in short shares. The arithmetic matters when tracking ASX stocks through a capital raising or a merger funded with new shares.

Shorted's [most shorted ASX stocks](/top) ranking is a starting point for finding a change. To explain it, follow the percentage back to its numerator, denominator and observation date.

## A percentage can fall while short shares stay unchanged

The calculation is:

```text
Short interest % = reported short shares ÷ total shares on issue × 100
```

Consider this hypothetical company. Assume its reported short position stays at 10 million shares while a completed capital raising increases issued shares from 100 million to 125 million. All figures refer to the same ordinary share class; there is no split or change in reporting coverage.

The percentage fell by **2 percentage points**, or **20% relative to its starting level**. Reported short shares fell by **0%**. These are three different statements, and all three are true.

There is less reported short exposure relative to the enlarged share base. There is no evidence in these two observations that the aggregate short quantity declined. Individual sellers could still have closed positions while others opened them.

The reverse can happen too. If the company cancels shares after a buyback, the same short quantity represents a larger fraction of the remaining shares. A rising percentage alone does not establish that additional short shares were sold.

## Separate covering from a change in the denominator

ASX defines covering a short position as buying the asset to cancel that position. That describes a transaction. A short-interest percentage describes a ratio at a point in time. [ASX glossary, “cover”.](https://www.asx.com.au/investors/investment-tools-and-resources/glossary)

Use the following hypothetical comparisons to see why the distinction matters. Each starts with 10 million reported short shares and 100 million issued shares, or 10% short interest.

<GraphTable title="Three routes to a lower short percentage" corner="Hypothetical comparisons">

| Later observation | Reported shorts | Shares on issue | Short interest | What changed |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| A | 8 million | 100 million | 8% | Short quantity declined |
| B | 10 million | 125 million | 8% | Issued capital increased |
| C | 11 million | 125 million | 8.8% | Shorts grew, but issued capital grew faster |

</GraphTable>

All three percentages fell. Only A shows a lower reported short quantity. C shows **more** short shares despite a lower short percentage.

Even A establishes only the change between two reported totals. It does not identify who bought, when they traded, or whether they changed their view of the business. For the separate distinction between transactions and outstanding positions, read [ASX short interest versus short-sale volume](/blog/short-interest-vs-short-volume-asx).

## Check when new shares entered the count

A capital-raising announcement and the date new shares enter a dataset need not coincide. ASX's market-capitalisation methodology describes a share count based on issuer disclosures and quotation timing. It generally includes issued ordinary shares whether quoted or unquoted, with separate treatment for some securities and structures. That methodology is useful context; it is not a substitute for the denominator in the ASIC row being checked. [ASX market-capitalisation methodology.](https://www.asx.com.au/markets/market-resources/market-capitalisation-methodology)

When investigating a discontinuity, open the company's announcements and look for the completed issue, allotment or quotation details. An announced intention to raise capital does not establish that the shares were already included on the date of the short-position observation.

Compare the shares on issue in the two source records. Do not divide an old short count by today's issued capital and describe the result as the historical percentage. That would combine observations from different dates.

Shorted's study of [what happens after ASX short interest peaks](/blog/what-happens-after-asx-short-interest-peaks) discusses a Sigma Healthcare example in its treatment of denominator changes. That qualification is part of interpreting an apparent unwind, not a reason to discard the history.

## Splits and consolidations change the unit of measurement

A share split increases the number of shares and proportionately reduces the price per share, before other market movements. A consolidation reverses the unit change. [ASX glossary, “split”.](https://www.asx.com.au/investors/investment-tools-and-resources/glossary)

Here is a simplified one-for-ten consolidation, assuming both quantities adjust consistently and nobody changes their economic position:

| Measure | Before consolidation | After consolidation |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Reported short shares | 10 million old shares | 1 million new shares |
| Shares on issue | 100 million old shares | 10 million new shares |
| Short interest | 10% | 10% |

The short count has fallen by 90%, but each new share represents ten old shares. Calling this “90% of shorts covered” would confuse a change in units with a trade.

This is why checking the numerator alone is insufficient around a split or consolidation. Confirm the corporate action and put both dates on a comparable basis before calculating a change in quantity. If the source records do not yet reconcile, leave the movement unexplained until they do.

## A five-minute check before calling it short covering

<Steps title="Check the dates, quantities and corporate actions">

1. **Record the two position dates.** Use the dates the observations describe. Shorted's [methodology](/methodology) explains the reporting lag; today's price move may fall outside the published position window.
2. **Compare the actual short quantities.** Did they decline, stay level or rise? Keep the exact figures beside the rounded percentages.
3. **Compare shares on issue.** An increased denominator may explain some or all of a percentage decline. If both quantities changed, report both.
4. **Read the corporate-action announcements.** Check the security class, new-share timing and any split or consolidation ratio.
5. **Reconcile the source records.** Use the [data hub](/data) and the dated ASIC report. If another website disagrees, work through [why ASX short-interest figures differ](/blog/why-asx-short-interest-data-differs).

</Steps>

ASIC's report page also flags limitations in submitted data and possible day-to-day variations caused by technical difficulties. A surprising change deserves a source check before a trading explanation. [ASIC short-position reports.](https://www.asic.gov.au/short-position-reports)

## Frequently asked questions

### Does falling short interest mean short sellers are buying back?

It can be consistent with covering, but the percentage alone cannot establish it. Check short shares, issued shares and any change in units. Aggregate totals also conceal offsetting activity: one seller can close while another opens.

### Can short shares rise while the short percentage falls?

Yes. In example C, shorts rise from 10 million to 11 million while issued shares rise from 100 million to 125 million. The percentage falls from 10% to 8.8% because the denominator grows faster.

### Does a capital raising make a short squeeze less likely?

The percentage calculation cannot answer that. A larger issued share count does not tell you how much stock holders will sell, whether borrow is available or what will happen to trading volume. Read the [days-to-cover guide](/blog/days-to-cover-asx) for the separate relationship between reported positions and trading volume.

### How should I describe a fall that I cannot explain?

Use the observation you can support: “Reported short interest fell from 10% to 8%; the cause has not been established.” If issued capital explains the change, say so. Reserve claims about sellers' trades or motives for evidence that addresses them.
