# Shorting Australian bank stocks

Short positions in Australia's four biggest banks have more than tripled in a year. ASIC's report for 15 October 2026 shows 262.2 million [CBA](/shorts/CBA), [Westpac](/shorts/WBC), [NAB](/shorts/NAB) and [ANZ](/shorts/ANZ) shares sold short, valued at $15.1 billion at that day's closes, against 71.4 million shares and $4.1 billion on 15 October 2025.

ASIC published the 15 October file on 21 October, four trading days after the position date, so every short-position figure here is at least six days behind the market. The dollar figures multiply each day's reported shares short by that day's closing price. They are the value of reported short positions, not a measure of anyone's exposure, risk or profit. What a [short position](/blog/what-are-short-positions) is and [how one is opened in Australia](/blog/how-to-short-a-stock-australia) are covered in Shorted's guides; this piece is about what the data on the banks shows.

Westpac has the most shares short and the highest percentage, 2.94% of its shares on issue. CBA has the largest value: its 43.9 million shares are fewer than half Westpac's 100.4 million but are worth nearly twice as much, because CBA closed at $156.15 on 15 October and Westpac at $35.71.

The increase is in the number of shares, not the price. On unadjusted closes, CBA, Westpac and NAB finished 15 October 2026 between 6% and 9% lower than a year earlier and ANZ 8% higher, so the value of the four positions (up 3.65 times) moved almost exactly with the share count (up 3.67 times).

Each column but the last is the final ASIC report of its month. The four did not move together:

- **CBA**'s position rose from one month end to the next in every month but June. Its largest rise came in May, from 1.64% to 2.10%.
- **Westpac** made the biggest monthly rise of the four all year in September: 2.14% to 2.80%, or 22.5 million more shares short in a month.
- **NAB** had the biggest monthly fall: 1.87% at the end of June to 1.29% at the end of July, 17.1 million fewer shares, before it rebuilt to 2.03% by 30 September.
- **ANZ** was lower at the end of February 2026 (0.63%) than at the end of October 2025 (0.82%), and most of its rise came after July. Its 1.63% on 15 October is its highest reading since March 2019.

## Records, stated carefully

ASIC's daily series begins with positions held on 16 June 2010. Measured over the whole of it, each bank's records tell a different story.

| NAB | 2.22% | 14 Oct 2026 | 69.2m | 14 Oct 2026 |
| ANZ | 3.46% | 10 May 2016 | 101.0m | 10 May 2016 |

</GraphTable>

- **NAB** holds both records, set on 14 October 2026. Its highs before 2026 were 1.98% and 52.3 million shares, on 16 May 2016. It passed the share count on 29 May 2026 and the percentage on 29 September, then set new highs in both measures on 10 of the 12 reports from 29 September to 14 October.
- **CBA**'s 2.65% on 14 October is its highest share of capital. It first went past its 2016 high (2.63%, 25 February) on 8 October; its 15 October reading, 2.62%, is back below it. In shares, February 2016's 45.0 million is still its most; 14 October's 44.3 million fell short.
- **Westpac**'s 101.5 million shares on 14 October is its largest count. It first went past its 2016 high (99.8 million, 10 May) on 9 October. Its percentage on 14 October, 2.97%, is still below 2016's 2.99%.
- **ANZ** holds neither. Its records, 3.46% and 101.0 million shares on 10 May 2016, are about twice its current levels.

Taken together, the four had 263.7 million shares short on 14 October, the most on any day since May 2016 but still below the 277.3 million of 10 May 2016.

<Callout type="note" title="A percentage record is not a share-count record">

ASIC's percentage divides the shares reported short by the shares on issue, and the banks' share counts move. CBA had 1,707.5 million shares on issue in February 2016 and 1,673.5 million in October 2026, so a smaller short position is a larger percentage. Westpac's count rose from 3,335.8 million in May 2016 to 3,420.3 million, so a record number of shares is not a record percentage. A record is only meaningful in the measure it was set in.

</Callout>

## What a bank short costs to hold

A short seller borrows the shares it sells, and the lender keeps the economic benefits of owning them, dividends included, as the [Reserve Bank's 2014 study of the lending market](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2014/dec/5.html) describes. A short position that is open over an ex-dividend date pays the lender an amount equal to the dividend: a [manufactured dividend](/glossary/manufactured-dividend), which the ATO calls a dividend equivalence payment.

The payment carries no franking credits. The ATO's [Taxpayer Alert TA 2018/1](https://www.ato.gov.au/law/view/document?docid=TPA/TA20181/NAT/ATO/00001) notes that borrowing shares from non-residents over a dividend is generally cheaper, because non-residents do not require a gross-up for franking credits when a dividend equivalence payment is made.

A position held over both ex-dividend dates in a year pays lenders roughly that share of its value. The borrow fee, which the borrower pays the lender, comes on top, and it does not appear in ASIC's data. The [cost of shorting guide](/blog/cost-to-short-asx-stock) covers borrow fees, margin and recall risk. Dividend amounts are from [CBA](https://www.commbank.com.au/about-us/investors/dividend-information.html), [Westpac](https://www.westpac.com.au/about-westpac/investor-centre/dividend-information/dividend-payment-history/), [NAB](https://www.nab.com.au/about-us/shareholder-centre/dividend-information) and [ANZ](https://www.anz.com/shareholder/centre/your-shareholding/dividend-information/).

The other constraint is liquidity. [Days to cover](/blog/days-to-cover-asx) divides the reported position by average daily volume, so it measures a position against the stock's turnover: the number of sessions buying it back would take if every share traded went to covering.

## What the data cannot tell you

<Callout type="warning" title="Positions, not people or reasons">

ASIC aggregates the short position reports it receives and [publishes the total for each product](https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/markets/short-selling/short-selling-reporting-short-position-reporting) four trading days later. In ASIC's words, "these reports will not contain short seller details". A reported short can be a hedge against another holding, one leg of a pair or arbitrage trade, or a stand-alone position, and the file does not say which. Positions below ASIC's reporting threshold do not have to be reported. Each number is a level on one day, not a flow and not a forecast.

</Callout>

## 2008, 2016 and the long record

During the global financial crisis, ASIC banned covered short selling in financial stocks, the banks among them, for eight months. It announced on Friday 19 September 2008 that naked short sales would not be permitted from the following Monday's open ([08-204](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2008-releases/08-204-naked-short-selling-not-permitted-and-covered-short-selling-to-be-disclosed)). By the open on Monday 22 September, it had widened the ban to covered short sales in all listed stocks ([08-205](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2008-releases/08-205-covered-short-selling-not-permitted)).

ASIC lifted the covered ban for non-financial stocks from 19 November 2008 ([AD08-65](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2008-releases/ad08-65-asic-lifts-ban-on-covered-short-selling-for-non-financial-securities)). It kept it for financial securities, defined as the S&P/ASX 200 Financials index, property funds included, plus five other APRA-regulated businesses, until 25 May 2009 ([10-86AD](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2010-releases/10-86ad-asic-releases-guidance-on-short-selling-obligations)). Most naked short selling stayed banned for good. ASIC's [2012 review](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2012-releases/12-233mr-asic-reports-on-gfc-short-selling-restrictions) (REP 302) found the measures "broadly met their regulatory objectives", while noting they may have contributed to "reduced liquidity and increased price volatility".

There is no position record from the ban. ASIC's first aggregated report covered positions held on [16 June 2010](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2010-releases/10-129ad-asic-starts-reporting-of-short-positions/), and the big banks' previous peak came in 2016, six years into the series. On 10 May 2016 ANZ had 3.46% of its shares reported short and Westpac 2.99%, both still their percentage records. NAB's 2016 high came six days later, at 1.98%, and CBA's on 25 February, at 2.63%.

[The Widow-Maker](/features/the-widow-maker), Shorted's feature on the bear case for Australian housing, covers the 2016 trade and the case made for it.
